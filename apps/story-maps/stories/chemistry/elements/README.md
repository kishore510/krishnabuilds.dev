# The Hunt for the Elements

Status: built. How the elements were found and the periodic table filled in, followed from laboratory to laboratory, 1669 to today, in 17 chapters: Brand's phosphorus at Hamburg, the four elements of Ytterby, Lavoisier's list at Paris, Davy's electric battery at London, Bunsen and Kirchhoff's spectra at Heidelberg, helium seen in the Sun at Guntur, Mendeleev's table at St Petersburg, gallium at Paris, scandium at Uppsala, germanium at Freiberg, the noble gases at London, Moseley's atomic numbers at Manchester, technetium at Palermo, the elements beyond uranium at Berkeley, Darmstadt's one-atom elements, the heaviest elements at Dubna and nihonium at Wako. 5 people cards (Lavoisier, Davy, Mendeleev, Moseley, Seaborg).

- `story.json`: the story data. It uses the `world` basemap and the shared art library, with no new glyphs. Routes show only for the current chapter. Dotted yellow lines show a sample travelling between laboratories (molybdenum from Berkeley to Palermo, berkelium from Oak Ridge to Dubna), not a person. The European places sit close together on this map, but no chapter shows two of them in full, so Europe's dot-near-pin warnings may stand.
- Source: every card is **History**.
- Native names: chapter 1 (kaltes Feuer), chapter 5 (Spektralanalyse) and chapter 10 (Bergakademie Freiberg), in German; chapter 2 in Swedish, chapter 3 in French, chapters 7 and 16 in Russian, chapter 17 in Japanese. The Mendeleev card gives his name in Russian.
- Sources and what is disputed: the discoverers' papers and Nobel lectures, IUPAC's reports on the discovery and naming of elements 104 to 118, and standard histories of chemistry. Brand called his substance 'cold fire'; the name phosphorus was applied to it later, so the card says it "became known as" phosphorus. Lavoisier's 33 substances are often said to include 23 true elements, counting oxygen, nitrogen (azote), hydrogen, sulphur, phosphorus, carbon and 17 metals; his "radicals" of muriatic, fluoric and boracic acids are not counted. Davy's 1808 metals were first got as amalgams or impure, which the card does not go into. Janssen is credited with first seeing the helium line at Guntur; other observers in India also recorded a yellow line that day, and Lockyer saw it independently in England in October 1868. The story of Mendeleev's dream is told secondhand and treated with caution, and Lothar Meyer's independent table is named. Winkler's first atomic weight for germanium was about 72.3; the card gives today's 72.6, which is "about" in either case. Element 43 had been claimed as 'masurium' by Noddack, Tacke and Berg in 1925; the claim was never confirmed, and the card follows the standard credit to Segrè and Perrier. The Dubna–Livermore team also reported element 113 in 2004 as a product of 115; IUPAC credited RIKEN. Seaborg and Oganessian are the two people honoured with an element in their lifetime.
