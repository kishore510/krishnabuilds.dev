# Building Molecules

Status: built. How chemists learned to build the molecules of life, followed from laboratory to laboratory, 1828 to today, in 14 chapters: Wöhler's urea at Berlin, Kolbe's vinegar at Marburg, Perkin's mauve in London, Kekulé's benzene ring at Ghent, Fischer's sugars at Würzburg, synthetic indigo from Ludwigshafen to Champaran, aspirin at Elberfeld, Woodward's quinine at Harvard, vitamin B12 with Eschenmoser at Zurich, Corey's retrosynthesis, the bark of the yew, the Taxol race, click chemistry and the medicines made in Hyderabad today. 5 people cards (Wöhler, Perkin, Woodward, Wani, Bertozzi).

- `story.json`: the story data. It uses the `world` basemap and the shared art library, plus two new glyphs drawn for this story: `benzene` (Ghent) and `pill` (Elberfeld, Hyderabad). Routes show only for the current chapter. Dotted yellow lines are news of a result travelling, not a person; the dashed blue line is synthetic indigo shipped to India. The European places sit close together on this map, but no chapter shows two of them in full, so Europe's dot-near-pin warnings may stand.
- Source: every card is **History**, except the last, on today's medicines, which is **Science**.
- Native names: chapter 1 (Harnstoff) and chapter 7 (Acetylsalicylsäure), in German.
- Sources and what is disputed: the chemists' own papers and Nobel lectures, and standard histories of chemistry. How far Wöhler's urea ended belief in a vital force (historians now say it faded over decades, and the card says so); Kolbe's first use of "synthesis" is given as "often credited"; Kekulé's dream story is his own, told 25 years later, and treated with caution; Arthur Eichengrün's claim to have directed the aspirin work is disputed; Woodward and Doering's 1944 quinine was a formal synthesis that relied on Rabe and Kindler's 1918 step, doubted until Robert Williams's group repeated it in 2007. The Champaran card gives the link between synthetic indigo and the planters' demands as context, not as the only cause. The Hyderabad card gives India's share of generic medicines as "a large share", not a figure, as estimates vary.
