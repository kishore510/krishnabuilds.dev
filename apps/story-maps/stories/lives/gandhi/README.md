# Mahatma Gandhi

Status: built. In Lives. The life of Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi, 1869 to 1948, in 27 chapters: birth in Porbandar, a child bride in Rajkot, law in London, a briefless barrister, twenty-one years in South Africa (Natal, the night at Pietermaritzburg, the Natal Indian Congress, the ambulance corps, Phoenix Settlement, the oath of 1906, the burning of the certificates, Hind Swaraj at sea, Tolstoy Farm, the Great March and the voyage home), then in India the Sabarmati ashram, Champaran, non-cooperation, the Salt March, the Round Table Conference, the Poona Pact, Sevagram, Quit India, the Aga Khan Palace, Noakhali, Calcutta on Independence Day and Birla House. 7 people cards.

- `story.json`: the story data. It uses the `world` basemap. Routes show only for the current chapter. Places in South Africa and in western India are closer together than a pin on this world map, so no route is drawn between them (the walk to Dandi has none). The only legs are the sea voyages between India, London and South Africa, which are bent so they read as voyages, not tracks; Cape Town is a hidden waypoint.
- No new art.
- Source: every card is **History**.
- Native names are in Gujarati (`gu`), with Hindi (`hi`) and Bengali (`bn`) set on the chapters and people that use them.
- Sources and what is disputed: Gandhi's An Autobiography: The Story of My Experiments with Truth and Satyagraha in South Africa, The Collected Works of Mahatma Gandhi, and biographies and histories by Judith Brown, Ramachandra Guha (Gandhi Before India; Gandhi: The Years that Changed the World) and Rajmohan Gandhi. Disputed or handled with care: his early writings in South Africa, which used racial slurs about Black Africans and sought separate treatment for Indians (see Ashwin Desai and Goolam Vahed, The South African Gandhi, 2015), which the cards do not hide but do not have room to argue; the Poona Pact, which Ambedkar said he signed under pressure; deaths in the Quit India movement and in Partition, given only as disputed estimates; the size of the 1913 march (roughly 2,000, figures vary); and the coining of "satyagraha", which grew from a reader's suggestion ("sadagraha") in Indian Opinion.
